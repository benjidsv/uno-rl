## What was built

- `uno/compiled/pimc.py`: exact **PIMC-infinity** on the toy world trees (`pimc_response`, `pimc_row`)
  and **PIMC-n** as a behavioural policy (`pimc_n`), over two axes -- belief weighted by the
  opponent's reach (`posterior`) or by the worlds' prior alone (`prior`), and each world solved
  two-sided (`minimax`) or as a perfect-information best response to the opponent (`response`).
- `uno/eval/search.py`: the agent where no tree exists -- `determinize_l1` (sample the opponent's
  hand *and* the pile from an observation), alpha-beta `search_value` / `search_q` with 9.5c's value
  network at the leaves, and the agents `PimcSearch` (PIMC proper) and `PimcRollout` (the
  fusion-free comparator).
- `uno/experiments/pimc.py`: `exact`, `pimcn`, `uno`, `d2`, `sweep`.
- Tests: `tests/compiled/test_pimc.py`, `tests/eval/test_search.py`.

## 1. Exact PIMC-infinity on the toys (`uv run uno pimc exact`)

The n -> infinity limit, computed over the whole belief. On a world tree every non-root arc has one
edge of probability 1 and `reach(...)[:, 2]` is the world's prior, so the perfect-information value
of each action is a *gather*, and neither the belief nor those values depend on PIMC's own policy:
the whole policy is **one global grouped argmax**, no backward recursion. 64 solves, **0 failed
checks**, every one within its pre-declared bound (`value <= BR L1`; `believed` equal to BR L3 under
`response` and to the two-sided value under `minimax`; `prior` never leaving a set without mass).

Gap to BR L1 in score points, best variant to worst:

| ruleset | best | worst | which is worst |
|---|---|---|---|
| `TOY` | 0.04 | 2.86 | `prior/minimax` |
| `TOY2` | 0.30 | 3.21 | `prior/minimax` |
| `TOY2_SKIP` | 0.15 | 2.20 | `prior/minimax` |
| `TOY_FULL` | 0.04 | **8.78** | `posterior/minimax` and `prior/minimax` |

Readings:

1. **The continuation rule dominates the belief.** `posterior/response` stays within 0.04-0.70
   points of BR L1 on every toy, seat and opponent (`prior/response` 0.09-2.92); `minimax` --
   classic PIMC, which assumes the opponent is clairvoyant too -- loses 0.15-3.21 on the forced
   toys and **8.4-8.8 on `TOY_FULL`**. Free play is where assuming a clairvoyant opponent hurts:
   PIMC plays scared when a voluntary draw is available.
2. **Inference is worth little against Random, and most where it should be.** `posterior` beats
   `prior` by 0.0-0.6 points against Random, but by up to **2.6** against the Heuristic at seat 1
   (`TOY2`; 2.0 on `TOY_FULL`): modelling the opponent's reach pays in proportion to how far that
   opponent is from uniform, which Random is not. It also *costs* exploitability -- on `TOY` against
   the Heuristic, `posterior/response` is the stronger row (0.7374 vs 0.7362) and the *more*
   exploitable one (2.91 / 4.03 against 0.81 / 0.34): fitting the belief to one opponent is itself a
   way of being exploitable.
3. **`prior/minimax` reads nothing about the opponent at all**, so it is literally the same policy
   whichever opponent it was built against, with identical exploitability. That is the honest
   analogue of a full-UNO PIMC, and it is a check rather than a curiosity.
4. **PIMC's tie-breaking is value-relevant, unlike a best response.** For a best response tied
   actions have equal continuation value, so any tie-break scores the same. A PIMC tie is a tie in
   the belief-weighted *perfect-information* score, and two tied actions can be worth different
   amounts in the real game. So "PIMC" is a family indexed by its tie-break: the quotient's
   orbit-uniform choice and a full tree's pure per-set choice differ by 0.01-0.44 points on `TOY`.
   The orbit-uniform one is canonical because it is the symmetric one, and its value was checked
   equal to the same policy lifted onto the full tree (16 of 16).

## 2. PIMC-n: the cost of a finite number of worlds (`uv run uno pimc pimcn`)

A PIMC-n player re-samples at every decision, and perfect recall means it reaches an information set
at most once per game, so what it follows is the behavioural policy
`pi_n(a | I) = P(argmax over n sampled worlds)`. That is estimated per set and then scored by **one
exact `evaluate`**, so these numbers carry no game-playing noise at all.

Gap to PIMC-infinity, score points (`posterior/response`):

| ruleset | n=1 | n=8 | n=64 |
|---|---|---|---|
| `TOY` | 0.86-4.34 | 0.27-1.04 | 0.02-0.11 |
| `TOY2` | 2.88-4.34 | 1.04-1.57 | 0.17-0.45 |
| `TOY2_SKIP` | 4.49-5.99 | 0.74-1.70 | 0.13-0.28 |
| `TOY_FULL` | 2.35-7.36 | 0.61-1.51 | 0.04-0.23 |

Sampling costs 2-7 points at n = 1 and is essentially paid off by n = 8-16, with the share of mass
on PIMC-infinity's own choice rising 0.95 -> 0.99. **This is the conclusion that does not transfer**
(section 3): a toy belief holds 2-23 consistent worlds at the decisions actually faced
(reach-weighted means: 2.4 on `TOY_FULL` up to 22.5 on `TOY2`), full UNO's holds astronomically
more, so the same n covers a vanishingly smaller share of it.

It converges to PIMC-infinity but **not always from below**: on `TOY` against the Heuristic at seat 1
it overshoots (-0.08 at n = 32, -0.11 at n = 64). PIMC-infinity maximises the belief-weighted
perfect-information score, which is not the true value, so the deterministic limit need not be the
best member of its own family. The pre-declared check is that the gap *shrinks*, never that it stays
positive.

## 3. The full-UNO budget sweep (`uv run uno pimc sweep 200`)

Raw score against Random, 200 EVAL deals per config, both seatings.

Cost, measured separately and without contention, as **one-core** ms per game: 137 at w = 4 d = 2,
263 at w = 8 d = 2, ~770 at the w = 32 d = 2 headline, against 4 for the Heuristic. Evaluation
parallelises 3.7-4.4x over 8 cores. Note that a per-decision cost does *not* extrapolate to a game:
`PimcSearch.policy` short-circuits whenever only one action is legal, which is a large share of a
real game, so multiplying a per-decision figure by the ~45-55 decisions per game overstates the
cost several-fold.

| row | seat 0 | seat 1 |
|---|---|---|
| PimcSearch w=2 d=2 | 0.4150 +- 0.0349 | 0.4200 +- 0.0350 |
| PimcSearch w=8 d=2 | 0.5950 +- 0.0348 | 0.5250 +- 0.0354 |
| **PimcSearch w=32 d=2** | **0.6950 +- 0.0326** | 0.5600 +- 0.0352 |
| PimcSearch w=8 d=3 | 0.5600 +- 0.0352 | 0.4900 +- 0.0354 |
| PimcSearch w=32 d=3 | 0.6600 +- 0.0336 | 0.5300 +- 0.0354 |
| PimcRollout w=8 r=1 | 0.4150 +- 0.0349 | 0.4000 +- 0.0347 |
| PimcRollout w=32 r=1 | 0.5750 +- 0.0350 | 0.5000 +- 0.0354 |
| PimcRollout w=8 r=4 | 0.4400 +- 0.0352 | 0.4800 +- 0.0354 |
| Heuristic | 0.5750 +- 0.0350 | 0.6700 +- 0.0333 |

Readings:

1. **Worlds help, a lot, and the ladder had not plateaued at w = 32.** Depth 2 goes
   0.415 -> 0.595 -> 0.695 at seat 0 for w = 2 -> 8 -> 32, far outside the +-0.035 standard errors.
   This sweep stopped at 32 only because that was the budget tried; section 3b extends the ladder to
   64 and 128 and finds it flat beyond 32, so the gains live entirely in the first few doublings.
2. **"Depth hurts" is *not* established by this table.** Depth 3 came out below depth 2 at both
   world counts (0.660 against 0.695 at w = 32, 0.560 against 0.595 at w = 8) while costing three
   times as much, but each of those differences is about **one standard error** of an unpaired
   comparison at 200 deals -- nowhere near the 3-sigma this project requires. Three mechanisms
   would all produce that sign and are not separated by it: deeper perfect-information search
   sharpens the *clairvoyant* estimate and so amplifies strategy fusion; deeper search also makes
   per-world values more extreme, which makes the argmax over few worlds noisier (in which case the
   penalty should vanish as worlds grow); and depth 2 against depth 3 evaluates the network at
   states with a different player to move, the classic odd-even search artefact. A powered test is
   in section 3a.
2. **A small world count is not merely imprecise, it is harmful.** At w = 2 (and for rollouts at
   w = 8, r = 1) PIMC scores *below* `PlayFirstRandom`. Choosing the argmax of a few noisy per-action
   estimates is biased towards whichever action's noise came out high, and unlike Random that bias
   happily picks a voluntary draw with a playable card in hand. The same winner's curse bit the D2
   estimator twice (section 6).
3. **Search beats rollouts at equal worlds** everywhere (w = 32: 0.695 / 0.560 against 0.575 / 0.500),
   and extra rollouts per world buy less than extra worlds.
4. **PIMC at its best beats the Heuristic at seat 0 (0.695 against 0.575) and loses at seat 1
   (0.560 against 0.670).** The Heuristic is the stronger seat-1 player on full UNO, the opposite of
   its toy profile (docs/context.md section 6.2).

## 3a. Does depth actually hurt? (`uv run uno pimc sweep 200 "" "s32x2,s32x4,heu"`)

200 EVAL deals, both seatings, w = 32, the two depths played on the **same deals**.

| row | seat 0 | seat 1 | one-core ms/game |
|---|---|---|---|
| PimcSearch w=32 d=2 | 0.6950 +- 0.0326 | 0.5600 +- 0.0352 | ~722 |
| PimcSearch w=32 d=4 | 0.6200 +- 0.0344 | 0.5000 +- 0.0354 | ~6785 |
| Heuristic | 0.5750 +- 0.0350 | 0.6700 +- 0.0333 | ~1.3 |

Paired **d4 - d2 = -7.50 +- 4.37 points (z -1.7)** at seat 0 and **-6.00 +- 4.68 (z -1.3)** at seat 1.

Readings:

1. **The odd-even leaf artefact is disfavoured.** Depth 4 has the same parity as depth 2, so if
   depth 3's dip had been a parity effect depth 4 should have returned to depth 2's level. It did
   not: it fell further, and the point estimates are monotone in depth at both seats
   (0.695 / 0.660 / 0.620 at seat 0 for d = 2 / 3 / 4).
2. **The harm is still not established at 3 sigma** (z -1.7, -1.3), so by the threshold used
   everywhere else here, "depth hurts" stays **unproven**. What *is* solid is that depth does not
   help: the 95 % interval for d4 - d2 at seat 0 is about [-16.1, +1.1] points, so the best case
   depth can claim is ~1 point of gain for **8.3x the cost** (1622 against 197 ms per game,
   wall-clock on 8 cores). Depth is dominated on cost-effectiveness without its harm being proven,
   which is enough to stop spending budget there and not enough to publish a mechanism.
3. **The pairing did not buy what was predicted.** The paired standard error (4.37) is barely under
   the unpaired one (~4.74), about 8 %. Sharing the world-sampling key couples the two agents only
   until their games diverge, and on a ~50-decision game that happens quickly -- unlike step 9's
   near-identical policy variants, which stay coupled for most of a game. The claim that these
   differences would be "tight" was wrong and has been removed from the tool's own output.
4. Still open, and **not** tested by this run: whether the depth penalty shrinks as worlds grow --
   the selection-noise explanation -- which needs depth comparisons at several world counts. (Whether
   more worlds keep helping is answered in section 3b: past 32, not measurably.)

## 3b. Do more worlds keep helping? (`uv run uno pimc sweep 200 "" "s32x2,s64x2,s128x2,heu"`)

200 EVAL deals, both seatings, depth 2, paired on the same deals.

| row | seat 0 | seat 1 | one-core ms/game |
|---|---|---|---|
| PimcSearch w=32 d=2 | 0.6950 +- 0.0326 | 0.5600 +- 0.0352 | ~722 |
| PimcSearch w=64 d=2 | 0.6100 +- 0.0346 | 0.5950 +- 0.0348 | ~1413 |
| PimcSearch w=128 d=2 | 0.7250 +- 0.0317 | 0.6200 +- 0.0344 | ~2993 |
| Heuristic | 0.5750 +- 0.0350 | 0.6700 +- 0.0333 | ~1.3 |

Paired against w = 32: **w = 64 is -8.50 +- 3.80 (z -2.2) / +3.50 +- 4.28 (z +0.8)**;
**w = 128 is +3.00 +- 3.75 (z +0.8) / +6.00 +- 4.46 (z +1.3)**.

Readings:

1. **The ladder has flattened by 32 worlds.** The early steps were large and unambiguous (2 -> 8 is
   about +18 points at seat 0, 8 -> 32 about +10); past 32 nothing reaches 3 sigma and the points are
   not even monotone. Quadrupling the worlds, and the cost, to w = 128 buys +3.0 / +6.0 points,
   neither significant. The honest summary is **no measurable gain beyond 32 worlds at this sample
   size**, with the intervals still admitting up to about +10 points at w = 128 seat 1. Section 3's
   "32 is not a measured ceiling" is now measured: it is close to one, for practical budgets.
2. **The w = 64 dip is read as noise, not a finding.** At z -2.2 it is the largest deviation in the
   table, but it is contradicted by w = 128 scoring above both, and one |z| > 2 among the four
   comparisons here -- let alone across this step -- is unremarkable. Treating it as real would need
   its own run.
3. **At equal compute, worlds beat depth.** w = 128 d = 2 (~2993 one-core ms per game) scores
   0.725 / 0.620 against w = 32 d = 3's (~3117 ms) 0.660 / 0.530, better at both seats for slightly
   less time, on the same first 200 EVAL deals. This is a comparison *across* runs rather than paired
   within one, so it is weaker than the depth test in section 3a, but it points the same way.
4. **The rng-prefix pairing helped, but less than predicted.** The configs share a world-sampling
   key, so w = 128's first 32 worlds *are* w = 32's, which was expected to make this comparison much
   tighter than the depth one. Measured, it removes 18-20 % of the standard error against depth's
   8 % -- better, but nowhere near step 9's 77-85 % for near-identical policy variants on a shared
   stream.

## 4. AIVAT on the PIMC row (`uv run uno pimc uno 300 32 2`)

300 EVAL deals per matchup at the w = 32 headline budget, both seatings, V = 9.5c's
heuristic-vs-random network (off-profile). **0 failed checks.** Score of the row, and the variance
ratio with corrections at chance and both players:

| matchup | raw s0 / s1 | AIVAT ratio s0 / s1 | deals for 3 pts, raw -> AIVAT |
|---|---|---|---|
| pimc vs random | **0.7033 / 0.5633** | 0.599 / 0.594 | 3433 -> 2058, 4047 -> 2402 |
| pimc vs heuristic | 0.5133 / 0.4633 | 0.619 / 0.542 | 4110 -> 2545, 4091 -> 2218 |
| pimc_rollout vs random | 0.5400 / 0.5000 | 0.682 / 0.591 | 4087 -> 2786, 4113 -> 2433 |
| pimc_rollout vs heuristic | 0.4067 / 0.3933 | 0.685 / 0.665 | 3970 -> 2719, 3926 -> 2613 |
| heuristic vs random | 0.5767 / 0.6433 | 0.408 / 0.524 | 4016 -> 1638, 3775 -> 1977 |

Readings:

1. **At w = 32 the search row overtakes the rulebot at seat 0 and stays behind at seat 1**
   (0.7033 against 0.5767 vs Random; 0.5633 against 0.6433). Head to head it is about even at seat 0
   (0.5133) and behind at seat 1 (0.4633). The seat asymmetry is the agent's, not the game's: full
   UNO has no measurable seat effect (context.md section 6.1).
2. **AIVAT works on a search row, but less well than on a rulebot**: 0.54-0.62 against the
   Heuristic's 0.41-0.52. The value function is the reason -- it was fitted on rulebot games and
   scores R^2 0.08-0.16 on these, so it is off-profile. AIVAT stays unbiased regardless (every
   AIVAT - raw z within +-3; the largest, pimc vs random seat 0, is -2.77).
3. **For every PIMC row the row correction is identically zero** (`action row +0.0000` at every seat
   and matchup), because these agents are deterministic given the information state. All of AIVAT's
   gain on them comes from chance and from the opponent. The Heuristic row is the contrast: its own
   action noise is worth removing (0.600 -> 0.575 at seat 0 when the row correction is added).
4. **This is the regime 9.5c predicted AIVAT would pay in.** Per *game* on one core, a w = 32 search
   costs ~722 ms against the Heuristic's ~1.3 ms (9.5c's 2.6 ms covers a pair, i.e. two games), so a
   search game is ~550x the price of a rulebot game. The corrections' ~30-80 ms are then 4-11 % of
   the cost while removing ~40 % of the variance -- the opposite of the rulebot case, where playing
   4,000 more deals is far cheaper than halving the variance of the 4,000 you have.

## 5. D2 with a search Q (`uv run uno pimc d2 150 32 2`)

Step 9.5a's "where does the row lose its points" where no tree exists: play the row, and at each of
its decisions ask a w = 32 search what it would have played instead. 150 EVAL deals per seat, score
points per game, **0 failed checks**.

| seat | row | total | drawn | wild | action | color | other |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | heuristic | **21.16 +- 2.06** | 0.10 | 5.24 (24.8 %) | **15.34 (72.5 %)** | 0.32 | 0.16 |
| 0 | pimc (control) | -3.33 +- 0.73 | -0.45 | -0.63 | -0.18 | -0.39 | -1.68 |
| 1 | heuristic | **21.29 +- 2.04** | 0.52 | 6.27 (29.4 %) | **14.54 (68.3 %)** | 0.38 | -0.42 |
| 1 | pimc (control) | -2.53 +- 0.73 | -0.38 | -0.86 | -0.05 | 0.17 | -1.41 |

Readings:

1. **Action-card timing and wild handling are 97 % of it** (72.5 + 24.8 at seat 0, 68.3 + 29.4 at
   seat 1), and unlike on the toys the split is nearly seat-symmetric. This is the full-UNO
   counterpart of step 9.5a's exact findings -- skip timing was 24-40 % of the Heuristic's gap on
   `TOY2_SKIP`, and wild *colour* choice 71 % of its seat-1 gap on `TOY` -- so the qualitative
   transfer holds and sharpens: the two decision kinds the toys flagged are where full UNO's rulebot
   loses too. The `draw with a play in hand` decision, absent from the forced toys and available at
   60 % of full-UNO decisions, contributes almost nothing (`drawn` 0.10 / 0.52).
2. **Reference quality shows up in the decomposition, not just the total.** With the w = 8 reference
   (a search that *loses* to the Heuristic) the totals were 11.6 / 12.6 and the kinds did not
   partition anything: `other` came out at -2.9 / -3.7 and `action` at 112 % of the total. At w = 32
   the negative components essentially vanish. A reference weaker than the row it grades produces
   noise dressed as attribution.
3. **The control is what licenses reading any of this.** A row that plays the search's own choices
   must score ~0, and it does (-3.3 / -2.5 against the Heuristic's +21). The small negative residual
   is understood: the control chooses with all 32 worlds while the reference's `best` chooses with
   only the 16-world selection half, so the control is very slightly the better chooser.

Still not a value decomposition -- a search Q is not a fixed policy's value function and nothing
telescopes -- so the shares are the finding and the totals are not "how many points the Heuristic
is behind".

## 6. Bugs found, and what they cost

All three were caught by a check written *before* the number was believed, and all three are now
regression tests.

| bug | symptom | cause |
|---|---|---|
| arc order assumed positional | `pimc_n` 21 score points off at `TOY` seat 1, and **worse with more sampled worlds** | the members of an L1 set are different worlds, each canonicalised by its own group element, so arc `j` of a member is not set-action `j` (3.9-23.8 % of arcs, worse at seat 1). Must map through `k.arc_ia` -- the trap `uno.compiled.lookup` documents for `tree.arc_action`, one level over |
| winner's curse, selection | a D2 total of 34-40 score points per game, i.e. a rulebot "worse" than a search that does not in fact beat it | selecting and valuing on the same worlds: `max_a Qhat` overshoots by the expected maximum of the noise at every decision, and a game's ~22 decisions accumulate it |
| winner's curse, control | the control row at **-32** points per game instead of ~0 | fixing the above by splitting the worlds is not enough: a control row that is *itself* the search must draw its worlds from a different seed, or its action is picked using the very half it is then valued on |

The control row is what caught the third: it exists precisely because a row that plays its own argmax
must score ~0, and it did not. After the fix it sits at -3.8 / -3.5, the residual being that the
control chooses with all n worlds while the reference's `best` chooses with only half of them.

**One pre-existing flaky test, found and fixed** (outside this step's own code).
`tests/game/test_symmetry.py::test_cache_round_trip` asserted `str(again) == str(tree)` between two
*independent* compiles, and `CompiledTree.__str__` prints the compile time to a tenth of a second, so
it fails whenever the two compiles land either side of a rounding boundary -- which happened here
under the CPU load of a concurrent sweep (1.1s against 0.9s). The cache round-trip itself was never
at fault: the `again.seconds == cached.seconds` half of the same assertion passed. Fixed by comparing
`str(...).split(";")[0]`, which is precisely the guard the neighbouring assertion in that file
already uses.

## 8. What is left on the table

None of this was tested. It is what a next attempt at the PIMC row should try, largest first -- and
it is why "more worlds and more depth both stopped helping" should **not** be read as PIMC being at
its ceiling. The method's ceiling is visible in section 1: with infinite worlds solved exactly,
PIMC-infinity lands within **0.04-0.70 points of BR L1** on the toys. Full-UNO PIMC is far from that,
so the gap lives in the approximations below, not in the idea.

1. **The continuation rule.** The full-UNO search solves each determinized world by **minimax**, i.e.
   assuming the opponent is clairvoyant too. Section 1 measured exactly that choice on the toys: it
   is the *worse* of the two rules by up to **8.8 points**, and worst on free play, which full UNO
   is. In every ladder matchup the opponent's policy is known exactly (Random, the Heuristic), so
   best-responding to it inside each world -- the `response` continuation -- is directly
   implementable and needs no training. This is the cheapest large lever available.
2. **The leaf value function.** At depth 2 almost all the signal is 9.5c's network at the leaves, and
   that network is off-profile for a search row (outcome R^2 ~0.15, fitted on rulebot games). This is
   the likeliest reason section 3b's world ladder flattened: more samples of a position that is
   evaluated badly buy nothing. The toys' near-optimal PIMC-infinity effectively has a perfect leaf.
3. **Inference.** `determinize_l1` splits the unseen cards uniformly, so the agent does no opponent
   modelling at all -- the `prior` belief. On the toys the `posterior` belief is worth up to 2.6
   points against the Heuristic (and ~0 against Random, which is uniform anyway).

Two notes for whoever picks this up:

- **The seat-1 deficit is partly a known wiring defect, not saturation.** PIMC scores 0.560 at seat 1
  against the Heuristic's 0.670 -- an 11-point asymmetry in a game with no measurable seat effect
  (`docs/context.md` section 6.1). Found while writing this up: `uno_section` and `sweep_section`
  give the agent `nets[0]` at *both* seats, while `d2_section` correctly uses `nets[seat]`. Both
  networks return seat-0 values, so it is not a sign error, but the seat-1 network was fitted on a
  different state distribution, so at seat 1 the search evaluates its leaves with a mismatched value
  function. Every seat-1 number in sections 3, 3a, 3b and 4 is therefore mildly pessimistic. Fixing
  it is a one-line change with a testable prediction -- the asymmetry should shrink -- and is the
  first thing to do in any follow-up.
- **"It did not help" is bounded by power.** Sections 3a and 3b are 200 deals at SE ~3.5 points, so
  they exclude *large* gains from worlds and depth, not moderate ones; a 3-point effect needs ~1,800
  deals even with AIVAT (section 4).
