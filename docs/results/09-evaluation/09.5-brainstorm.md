## 1. What step 9 established

**The toy's absolute numbers belong to its scoring rule; its relative numbers are
robust.** Rescoring exhaustion moves the Nash seat gap by up to 13 points on the
forced toys, and both alternatives degenerate on free play (under "draw" the
trailing player stalls into a tie; under "stuck" Random becomes 40+ points
exploitable). The orderings survive every scoring: the Heuristic leaves ~5 points
from seat 0 and ~13 from seat 1, it is weakest from seat 1 and on the skip toy,
and L1 ≤ L2 ≤ L3 holds everywhere. Consequences: seat gaps and exploitability are
"under the rule", never compare a checkpoint against a number from another
scoring; and step 9.5 gets three exact games per forced-play tree for free.

**The Nash strategy is not unique, and that costs up to half a point.** Cells that
pit a fixed agent against Nash move ±0.1 on the forced toys and ±0.5 on the free
ones across CFR variants. Exploitability uses only the Nash value, so it is
unaffected. Report "vs Nash" cells with that bar.

**On these toys, exploiting Random and being unexploitable are nearly the same
policy.** With the least exploitable fill-in, the best response to Random is
1.0-1.6 points exploitable per seat on forced toys and 2.6-3.1 on `TOY2_FULL`.
This is a limitation of the toys as an RL testbed, not a fact about UNO: a policy
trained against Random will look near-Nash, so the toys can barely separate
"learned to exploit" from "learned to be safe". Only `TOY2_FULL` has room. The
fill-in problem itself is an artefact of *tabular* best responses; a network has
a policy everywhere, so exploitability is well defined for RL rows.

**The deal model is verified to about half a point, which is marginal.** At 20,000
seeds the check sees a 0.5-point bias at 2-3σ; the step 8.9 bug moved rows by up
to 0.4. Rerun once at 100,000 seeds (minutes).

**Splits corrected.** The excess of split pairs over independent games (13-29
points) is the deal's footprint; only 4 % of `TOY` worlds are decided before
anyone moves.

**Pairing on full UNO is the practical result, with two readings.** Common random
numbers make two variants of one policy play the identical game until their
first differing decision, so the paired difference only carries the variance of
the diverged tail.

| comparison on full UNO | deals for 3 points, 3σ, 80 % power |
|---|---|
| two variants of one policy, shared rng stream | 1,300-1,800 |
| same variant on its own stream | 4,000-5,700 |
| Heuristic vs Random, any design | 6,000-8,000 |

Training progress can be tracked cheaply if checkpoints share a stream. Ladder
placement against Random or the Heuristic still costs ~7,000 deals per seat, but
the transfer run played 24,000 full-UNO games in 39 s, so that budget is 25 s of
wall clock for rulebots: iterations 1-4 lacked a budget, not compute. AIVAT's job
on full UNO is therefore the *expensive* rows (search agents, large networks) and
per-decision diagnostics. The variants are proxies (the doc says so); the fix is
to add to `compare` the share of pairs whose histories diverged and the mean
decision index of divergence, a statistic that predicts the variance ratio and is
measurable for real checkpoints on the fly. Tickets help 3-20 % and never hurt:
adopt for evaluation, not the lever.

## 2. Section D of the step 9 review: verdicts and assumptions to check

**D1, a range with several ceilings: yes, mostly presentation (step 10).** The
two-sided values are not ceilings for an L1 agent (on `TOY` they sit *below* the
Nash L1 value): place them off the range as "what the game is worth when nothing
is hidden". The real ceilings are BR L1 against the training opponent
(fixed-opponent rows) and the Nash value plus exploitability (self-play rows). A
tuned Heuristic is worth it as a *transfer test* (section 3), not as a baseline,
and needs a small parameterised rulebot (the current one has one parameter).

**D2, edge by decision: the most valuable item, with an exact formulation the
review does not use.** Performance-difference lemma:

    V^BR - V^π = Σ_h P^BR(reach h) · [ Q^π(h, a_BR(h)) - V^π(h) ]

with Q and V under the agent's profile. Exact and additive, so grouping histories
by decision kind sums to the total gap ("swap in the best response only at kind
K" is not additive: interactions). Cost: a values pass under the agent, a reach
pass under the best response. The mirror version (the agent's reach × its regret
under the best response's values) is also exact; where both agree the attribution
is robust. On full UNO with sampled trajectories, a critic V and a search-based Q,
the same sum is an estimator whose terms are AIVAT's decision-node corrections:
build AIVAT first and this tool reuses it.

**D3, memory: yes, cheap on `TOY`, as nested keys.** K0 = Markov state (hand,
top, colour, sizes, drawn card); K1 = K0 + discard multiset; K2 = K1 + a
hand-crafted void summary ("opponent drew while colour c was current"); L1 = full
history. The gaps between the four say what the RL observation needs. To check: a
coarse set must not span levels on the compiled tree, so include the level in the
key; the greedy backward pass on an imperfect-recall key is a lower bound, and a
few rounds of policy iteration with own reach included tighten it; on the
quotient tree the coarse key must be canonicalised under the group, so start on
the full `TOY` tree (cached). Do not over-read: the value of history in a
6-decision game says little about a 44-decision one.

**D4, the metric for fixed-opponent training: agree.** Gap to BR L1 against that
opponent for fixed-opponent rows, exploitability for self-play rows. Milliseconds.

**D5, shaping: the review is right and can be sharpened.** With no discount and a
zero terminal potential, potential shaping adds exactly −Φ(s0) to every return.
With the L3 value of the deal as Φ, that is AIVAT's deal correction: it *removes*
deal luck from the return rather than adding noise, and the critic gets a less
variable target. The L3 potential and AIVAT's control variate are one object. Test
inside D6 with three potentials (exact L1, L2, L3 values).

**D6, the ablation (tabular / PPO with exact L1 critic / plain PPO): this is step
11's design. Do it.** Tabular on 12k canonical sets is trivial. Every learner must
go through `Symmetrized`, otherwise the exact cells need the full tree.

**D7, the mechanism questions (pile vs ranks, mutual information): skip.** Nothing
downstream depends on them; the full-UNO analogue of L2 − L1 will be measured
directly by PIMC with true versus sampled worlds.

## 3. Step 9.5: what transfers, what does not, how to quantify it

The distinction that organises everything: a sampled estimator has a **bias** and
a **variance**. Bias needs an exact reference, so it is a toy question. Variance
is measured directly on the games it is used on, so it is a full-UNO question and
needs no transfer. Transfer is only a problem where the toy is asked to *predict*
something about full UNO.

**AIVAT has nothing to transfer.** Unbiased for any V, provided the chance model
in the corrections is the true one. The toys prove the implementation: constant V
reproduces raw; exact V with corrections at chance and both players gives zero
variance; the three scorings; pile versus tickets; a stochastic opponent with and
without opponent corrections; the exact AIVAT variance by one backward pass
carrying E[X] and E[X²] per node. On full UNO the variance reduction is a
bootstrap CI over held-out games. Design points:

- V may use **full information**, since it is only a control variate. An L3
  critic fitted on dev-seed games is cheap, its quality is an R² on held-out
  games, and it costs orders of magnitude less than a PIMC value at every state.
  Make that the full-UNO V.
- With exact V and all corrections the estimate is the constant root value *of
  the chance model*, so a wrong deal model passes the toy check. Also compare the
  AIVAT mean with the raw mean (model-independent). On full UNO the reshuffle
  order is a hash of the seed (independent in practice); tickets make every draw
  uniform by construction.
- The one toy-to-full prediction: the curve of variance reduction against V
  quality (exact + noise σ, L1 set values, another profile's values, a fitted
  critic). Place the full-UNO critic on it by its R², then check directly.

**PIMC is where bias is real and grows with game length** (strategy fusion,
non-locality). Validate on the toys against exact BR L1 and BR L2 per number of
worlds, with and without opponent-reach weighting (without it PIMC does no
inference). The toy bias is a lower bound of the full-UNO bias, so PIMC on full
UNO is a strong row, never the ceiling. It is also the only expensive thing: n
worlds × a search per decision is 100-1000× slower than a rulebot, a few hundred
games per matchup, which is exactly where AIVAT pays. **Defer MCTS**: depth-limited
search inside PIMC covers the need; IS-MCTS would be validated the same way later.

**Quantifying transfer, three instruments:**

1. **A transfer curve.** Two or three intermediate rulesets between `TOY2_FULL`
   and `FULL_UNO`, sampled only, chosen to match full UNO's decision mix at game
   lengths near 12, 25 and 44 actions. On each, measure what is directly
   measurable: mirrored correlation, paired variance ratio with divergence
   statistics, AIVAT gain with a fitted critic, PIMC-n against the Heuristic
   paired. If each quantity moves smoothly with length from the exact toys to
   full UNO, the toy-validated end anchors the curve.
2. **Conclusion transfer, now cheap.** Distil rules from BR L1 on `TOY2_FULL`
   (when to draw voluntarily, when to hold the wild) into Heuristic variants and
   test them on full UNO with shared-stream paired comparisons at ~1,500 deals
   each; same for tuned-Heuristic parameters. The only test of "toy skill is UNO
   skill" that needs no exact full-UNO value.
3. **A full-UNO exploitability column.** Exact is impossible; local best response
   (Lisý & Bowling 2017), a one-step-lookahead exploiter that sees the opponent's
   hand and policy, is a standard cheap lower bound. Step 11 rather than 9.5.
